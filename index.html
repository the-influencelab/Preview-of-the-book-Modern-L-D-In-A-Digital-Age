Who Wrote This, And Why It's Not A Conventional Book

I began travelling at a very young age. In 1993, at twenty, I lived and worked in Ibiza. I lived with locals, ate their food and immersed myself in their way of life. Life was far more connected and interactive then. There were no phones in our pockets, just people, sunshine and conversation.

My job was selling excursions and nights at the top clubs on the island. On paper that sounds easy. Everyone is on holiday, everyone is happy, everyone wants a good time. In reality it was one of the most competitive places I have ever sold in. Every bar, every corner and every stretch of beach had someone selling the same nights, the same boat trips and the same promises. The air smelt of sun cream, salt and cheap cocktails, and every tourist had been stopped ten times before they reached me.

That is where I learnt my differentiator. If I sounded like everyone else, I was invisible. So I stopped selling the ticket and started selling the moment. I learnt to paint visual pictures: the sun dropping into the sea at San Antonio, the bass coming up through the floor as the doors opened, the boat anchored in a turquoise cove with nobody else around. When people could see it, they could feel it, and when they could feel it, the decision was already made.

I learnt to listen for people's soft spots. The couple celebrating something and wanting it to be special. The group of friends who would never get this week together again. The person who had saved all year for this one holiday. Each of them needed to hear something different, and what resonated with one would leave the next cold. I learnt that very quickly, because rejection on a busy strip is instant and honest.

I learnt to keep choice simple. Never ten options, only two. Too much choice and people freeze and walk away. Two options and they compare, decide and feel good about it. Then, once they had said yes, I learnt how to upsell with care: the better table, the boat with lunch included, the add on that made the night more memorable. It was never pushing. It was helping someone have the holiday they had pictured in their head.

My closing techniques there were fabulous, and I did not learn them from a book. I learnt them from hundreds of conversations in the heat, reading faces, adjusting in the moment and trying again. Everything I later built into my sales methodology, PROSPER, has its roots on that island: preparing, building rapport, finding the real need, painting the picture and resolving with confidence. Looking back, Ibiza taught me the first lesson of this book. People do not buy information. They buy what resonates with them.

At thirty one I sold my house and moved to Thailand, and I went back at thirty six to train as a TEFL teacher. Years later I lived on Koh Samui and Koh Phangan. There I became familiar with a slower, healthier pace of life, where people lived in communities and thought like a community. The Thai people laughed more than any community I had ever met.

It was there that I met my best friend, Viv. A former nurse turned teacher, with her son Ren, an amazing young boy, so intelligent, our next David Attenborough. Viv lived in the middle of the forest, close to the sea. She smelt of coconut oil and had the most amazing curly locks. If you think I am outspoken and direct, you should have met her. Sadly, Viv died of a heart attack a decade ago, and I miss her every day. She taught me so much about kindness, community and her love of animals. Part of her will live within me forever, and sometimes I still ask Viv what I should do, because she always had my best interests at heart.

Then there was California. I lived and worked in San Diego and travelled across the Midwest as head of sales for the US. I did a stint at Apex in Las Vegas, where the funniest old reps took me under their wing. I spent a day in Silicon Valley with the most intelligent Vietnamese minds I have ever met, and in Fort Lauderdale I saw the inside of some of NASA's work. I still pinch myself.

Of the ninety countries I have visited, my favourite place on earth is Colombia. I fell for it hook, line and sinker, and I have thought a lot about why. It is the energy. I always say energy is our first language, and in Colombia you can sense it straight away: the sincerity, the hardship, the growth and the kindness. Social status matters less there. People work together and they work hard.

I learnt Spanish in the north, in the indigenous regions, and spent time with the communities of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta. The air there is thick and green. It smells of wet earth, wood smoke, ripe mango and coffee drying in the sun. Colombia is one of the most biodiverse countries on earth, and you feel it in your body before your mind can name it. Hummingbirds, butterflies the size of your hand, cloud forest falling down to the Caribbean. Nothing lives alone there. Every plant, insect and root is part of something bigger.

When your material objects are taken away and you step outside the social structure we know, you see why we are here, and why we make so many mistakes while we live inside a structure built on such deep conditioning.

Why this matters for learning

Most of what I learnt in those places, I did not learn consciously. Nobody sat me down and taught me how to build trust with a stranger, how to slow down, or how to read a room in a language I barely spoke. I absorbed it. The brain learns most powerfully when we are stretched: outside our comfort zone, but not so far that we shut down. That is the edge of the Window of Tolerance, and it is where real growth happens. Comfort keeps us the same. Panic closes us down. The stretch is where we change.

Being stripped back to basics also brought me face to face with my shadow, in Carl Jung's sense. Jung described the shadow as the parts of ourselves we push out of sight because they do not fit the picture we want others to see. Without status, routine or the usual props, there is nowhere to hide them. That is uncomfortable, and it is also where honesty begins. I believe that leaders and L&D professionals who have done some of their own shadow work hold far safer, braver rooms than those who have not.

Why this shapes how I work

This is why my methods are built on resonance and relatability. People do not change because they were told to. They change when they recognise themselves in what they are shown. A slide about a made up company rarely moves anyone. A scenario that sounds like their own Monday morning, their own difficult conversation, their own team, stops them in their tracks, because the brain pays attention to what feels personal and real.

Living inside other cultures taught me to listen for what is underneath the words. In Ibiza it was the soft spot behind the holiday. In Thailand it was the laughter that held a community together. In Colombia it was the quiet respect people gave each other and the land. That listening is what I bring into every room now. It helps me reduce bias, notice who is not being heard, and bring equilibrium to a room where people start from very different places, different generations, different roles and different confidence.

It is also why I see learning as an ecosystem. In the Sierra Nevada, in a Thai fishing village, and in a forest where the trees share through one underground network, nothing thrives alone.

The indigenous communities I spent time with taught me that the root of success is the network. There is a deep understanding of, and gratitude towards, the ecosystem that keeps everyone alive. There is less greed and more nurturing. People are honest and straightforward with each other. The earth is recognised for what it gives, and resources are used sustainably, because everyone knows that what is taken without care will not be there for the next generation.

They keep things simple. There are no fads. They are modern in what they choose to adopt, but they know what works, and what works is protected. Not one person is responsible for the forest and the land. Everyone is. There is a chief who also works alongside everyone else, who communicates openly, and who is praised and respected for it. It is simple, but it is very clear.

Organisations are the same. Too often we chase the next trend, the next platform or the next programme, and lose sight of what already works. Too often learning is handed to one department and everyone else steps back. What I saw in those communities is what I want for every workplace: a living system where knowledge is shared freely, where good practice is protected, where leaders work alongside their people rather than above them, and where everyone feels responsible for the health of the whole.

Learning is not the sole responsibility of L&D. It is a workplace community, with leaders at the centre.

I'm not a conventional thinker, and I don't believe conventional thinking is how change ever actually occurs. I'm a firm believer in being bold with data, stripping out as many PowerPoints as we possibly can, and using AI in the right place, with the right thinking, and the right people around the table before it arrives, not after.

L&D is not solely responsible for learning outcomes, and this book spends real time exploring what it looks like to let go of that assumption, to let people in, and to hold our own thinking with genuine novice curiosity rather than expert certainty.

Why this book, and why now

What has changed in how we learn

The job changed faster than the research. These four ideas run underneath every chapter.

  • The map is out of date

    Nothing new since 2023

    The last full CIPD learning at work survey was in 2023, before AI changed the job. Back then, 5% of learning professionals said they used AI tools. Nothing substantial has been written in this space since. A new study is under way, and until it lands we are working from a picture taken before everything moved.

    Introduction

  • Self regulation first

    The start of every leadership programme

    A leader who is regulated can think, listen and make a fair call under pressure. One who isn't goes reactive or numb, and the team follows. Self regulation and the Window of Tolerance belong at the front of all leadership and management training. Left out, we leave it to chance.

    Chapter 1

  • Adult learning theory

    Adults learn when it is real

    Knowles showed adults are self directed, experience driven, problem centred and intrinsically motivated. Three things he could not have anticipated now shape every room: digital distraction, post pandemic identity disruption, and five generations with five worldviews in one session.

    Chapter 14

  • Identity

    The gap nobody budgets for

    Identity Lag is the gap between who you were and who the moment now needs you to be. Every promotion, restructure and AI driven change opens it. The technology gets a training budget. The identity gap usually gets silence, until it has already cost something.

Part Two · The Ecosystem

Learning as a collective

The book looks at an organisation the way you would look at a forest. Strategy sits in the canopy, L&D is the tree, and every department is tangled together in the roots. Growth runs root up, so the work starts underground.

  • The shift

    From order taker to assertive architect

    A manager asks for resilience training, so a resilience workshop gets booked. The team's real problem is a manager who never gives feedback. An architect walks the land first, asks what is really going on, and designs with the people who will live inside it.

    Six weeks later, the same request comes back. Unless someone asked the harder question.

  • Commercial assertiveness

    Firm on the outcome, warm with the people

    Passive goes along with the brief to keep the peace. Aggressive pushes a view without listening. Assertive sits in the middle, starts with what the problem is costing in time, turnover, complaints or lost sales, and agrees what success looks like before anything is designed.

    “I can see why you have asked for this, and here is what I think will actually work.”

  • Collective decisions

    Bring the problem into the room

    A sales dip is often a handover problem between two departments that do not talk. Fishbone days put the people closest to it around one diagram: people, process, systems, environment, management and measures. It nearly always shows training is only part of the answer.

    Decided together, owned together.

  • The network

    Where the real learning moves

    The new manager who gets honest advice from someone in another department who went through the same thing. The product change that reaches customer service before the customer does, because someone picked up the phone. None of it shows on an org chart.

    Traditional L&D measures the trees. It rarely tends the network.

  • The canopy

    Behaviour before KPIs

    A senior team decides it needs a new capability, and on the way down it gets tied to a number that can be reported back up. A target gets set. A behaviour was needed. Only one of those is easy to measure, and it is rarely the one that matters.

    The canopy gives you the view. The ground gives you the truth.

  • Leaders at the centre

    Everyone owns the learning

    When learning is handed to one department, everyone else steps back. Collective teams work the other way: knowledge is shared freely, good practice is protected, and leaders work alongside their people at the centre of the network, not above it.

    The whole forest feels it when one part is missing.

Learning is not the sole responsibility of L&D. It is a workplace community, with leaders at the centre.

Part Three · The Drift to Drive Framework™

The five courage behaviours

Pushing back on a senior request, saying training may not be the answer, asking to observe before designing anything. All of it takes courage. Drift to Drive measures five behaviours on a continuum from order taking to architect.

  1. Deep DriftOrder taking
  2. Passive DriftAware, not acting
  3. Aware DriftThe turning point
  4. Early DrivePushing back
  5. Full DriveThe Architect

  • 1 of 5

    Commercial Courage

    Walking into the room ready to talk about the business before anyone asks. Saying no to a request that won't move it, and yes to the harder one that will.

    • Framing solutions in business terms
    • Feeling steady with senior leaders
    • Measuring behaviour, not attendance
    • Diagnosing before delivering

    In practice: baseline, fishbone, pilot, prove, then scale.

  • 2 of 5

    Relational Courage

    Giving the feedback rather than softening it into nothing. Telling the senior stakeholder the uncomfortable truth instead of managing around them.

    • Challenging a brief early
    • Holding an uncomfortable room
    • Stretching learners with care
    • Staying curious when pushed back on

    In practice: “I'm noticing some hesitation in the room. What's not being said?”

  • 3 of 5

    Creative Courage

    Building with others instead of arriving with a finished answer made at a desk. We predict around half of people will help when asked. In the Stanford research, 84% did.

    • Designing with the people who use it
    • Testing early, learning from what breaks
    • Asking for help more than feels comfortable
    • Using AI after the thinking

    In practice: invite people outside learning into design from day one.

  • 4 of 5

    Identity Courage

    Knowing who you are under pressure, especially when the role is asking you to become something you haven't grown into yet. We act our way into a new identity. We don't think our way there.

    • Describing your values in one minute
    • Holding your ground when L&D is undervalued
    • Knowing what makes you distinctive
    • Deciding where your line is on AI

    In practice: when a role changes, name the lag out loud.

  • 5 of 5

    Deep Courage

    Looking honestly at your own reactions rather than only managing everyone else's. The quietest of the five, and the one that decides whether the other four are real or performed.

    • Noticing your body under pressure
    • Naming the patterns you repeat
    • Investing in your own development
    • Using AI ethically

    In practice: you cannot take someone somewhere you have not been yourself.
